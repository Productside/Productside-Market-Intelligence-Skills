# The Act Layer

Frameworks consume fused evidence. They do not manufacture it. If a
quadrant, force, or cell has no cited signal behind it, the honest
entry is "no evidence found," not a plausible sentence.

If a dedicated skill exists for one of these frameworks
(`swot-analysis`, `porters-five-forces`, `ansoff-matrix`,
`tam-sam-som-calculator`, `pestel-analysis`,
`positioning-statement`, `ps-positioning-statement`), hand the fused
evidence to it rather than re-deriving the framework here. The sections
below carry the discipline rules that keep those frameworks honest when
they run on intelligence rather than on opinion.

---

## Market Landscape Scan

Run this **before** sizing or positioning, when you are new to a market
and do not yet know who is in it.

Sequence: scope -> how the market segments -> player map -> dynamics ->
whitespace.

**Segment by how buyers see the market, not by vendor marketing
categories, and note where the two disagree.** An analyst quadrant is a
map someone else drew for their own purposes. Where the buyer's mental
model and the vendor category diverge, that divergence is itself a
finding.

Cap the player map at 12. Beyond that you are cataloguing, not mapping.

The player map has four buckets and skipping the last three is the
usual failure:

- **Direct players** -- selling the same thing to the same buyer
- **Adjacent players** -- one product decision away from entering
- **Substitutes and non-consumption** -- spreadsheets, services,
  in-house builds, and doing nothing. Non-consumption is usually the
  market leader and almost never appears on competitive slides.
- **Emerging entrants** -- funded, hiring, pre-revenue

**Dynamics** gets four named reads, not a paragraph of atmosphere:

1. Where the money is
2. Where the momentum is
3. Consolidating or fragmenting
4. Technology and regulatory shifts in play

**Whitespace and dead zones.** Name both. A gap nobody serves is
sometimes an opportunity and sometimes a graveyard with a fresh coat of
paint. Say which you think it is and why.

Feeds: competitive snapshot (which players to profile), sizing (what to
count), positioning (what category you are in).

## Competitive Research Snapshot

Just-enough profiles on a named competitor set, plus a comparison
matrix and a "so what."

**Competitor selection rule.** Use the competitors the user provides.
If none are provided, identify the top 3. Use 4 only if clearly
needed. If the user names more than 4, ask which three appear in lost
deals most often and profile those. Pick by who shows up in deals, not
by who is largest. The matrix schema assumes three columns, so an
inflated set breaks the diff.

Per competitor: what they sell, to whom, positioning in their own
words, pricing posture, notable strengths, notable exposure, and the
last material thing they did with a date.

The comparison matrix compares on **dimensions the buyer uses**, not
dimensions you win on. If every row favors you, the matrix is marketing,
not intelligence. Include an **evidence quality** row: for each
competitor, how good is the sourcing behind their column. A matrix that
hides which column is guesswork is worse than no matrix.

Close on "so what", with counts: 3 implications, 2 risks, 2
opportunities, 3 assumptions to validate.

## Voice-of-Customer Mining

Reviews, app stores, forums, support communities, and social. Mine for
unmet needs and competitor weak points, with quoted evidence.

Structure: scope -> need themes -> competitor weak points -> switching
triggers -> so what.

**Need themes must be solution-free.** Four to eight words describing
what the person was trying to accomplish, not the feature they asked
for. "Cannot reconcile numbers across two systems" is a need. "Wants a
CSV export" is a request.

**Label every theme's frequency:** recurring across sources /
concentrated in one source / isolated. A vivid isolated complaint and a
recurring cross-source pattern are not the same evidence, and a theme
list that does not distinguish them will send a roadmap chasing one
angry reviewer.

**Note each source's bias.** Reviewers skew negative. Vendor
communities skew loyal. App store ratings skew bimodal. Public voice is
evidence with a known skew, weighted toward the angry and the vocal.
Themes are hypotheses to validate, not verdicts.

**Quote discipline.** Real quotes, with the platform and date. Never
compose a representative quote. If you cannot cite it, describe the
pattern and label it Inference.

**Switching triggers** are the highest-value output: the specific event
that made someone leave. Renewal, an outage, a price increase, a
reorg, a champion departing. Those are your entry points and your churn
early-warning list.

## TAM/SAM/SOM

Bottom-up, cited, with sensitivity. The recipe lives in
`disciplines.md` under GEOINT/DEMOINT. The discipline rules:

- Every input gets a source and a date. Numbers without provenance are
  the single most common way a business case dies in front of finance.
- Validate against two independent analyst reports. If they disagree by
  3x, report both and say so. Do not pick the flattering one.
- Capture rate for SOM comes from FININT, not optimism: [TARGET]
  revenue divided by claimed customer count is a deal size reality
  check.
- **SOM carries a horizon and a benchmark.** Realistic capture over 3
  to 5 years, benchmarked against named comparable companies in the
  space. A capture rate with no horizon and no comparable is a wish.
- Express every layer in **both currency and number of customers**.
  The customer count is what exposes an unbelievable model fastest.
- State pricing assumptions with benchmarks, and adoption or
  penetration assumptions with justification. Name the demand signals
  that support them.
- Show best / base / worst with the assumption that moves each. The
  sensitivity is the deliverable; the point estimate is decoration.
- State the method as either top-down-validated or bottom-up-built.
  Never blend them silently.

Output order: executive summary -> TAM/SAM/SOM breakdown -> key
assumptions -> sensitivity -> sources. Sizing runs get a 4-question
budget rather than 3, because the constraint set is wider.

Anti-pattern to refuse: "1% of a $50B market." That is not a model,
it is a wish with arithmetic.

## SWOT

**Quadrant discipline is the whole game.** Strengths and weaknesses are
internal and current. Opportunities and threats are external and not
controllable.

Two failure modes to catch by name:

- A competitor's product filed as a weakness instead of a threat.
- **An "opportunity" that requires them to act is a strategy, not an
  opportunity.** Flag it and move it. This is the most common way a
  SWOT quietly becomes a wish list.

Max 5 entries per quadrant. Every entry carries a source and a label.
**Customer voice weighs heaviest in weaknesses** -- what your own users
say about you outranks internal opinion about yourself.

Rank within each quadrant on one of four bases, stated: defensibility
(strengths), exploitability (weaknesses), fit to their strengths
(opportunities), likelihood times damage (threats).

**The crossings are the "so what."** A SWOT without them is a list.

- **S-O:** which strength lets you take which opportunity, and what to
  do this quarter
- **W-T:** which weakness gets exposed by which threat, and what
  protects it
- Optionally S-T (defend) and W-O (invest to qualify)

## Porter's Five Forces

Industry structure, not company comparison. Rate each force weak /
moderate / strong with documented signals behind the rating.

1. Competitive rivalry
2. Threat of new entrants
3. Threat of substitutes
4. Buyer power
5. Supplier power

Four rules that keep it useful:

- A force rating without a cited signal is a mood. "Buyer power:
  strong" means nothing. "Buyer power: strong -- three of five named
  buyers ran competitive bake-offs in the last cycle, per [source]"
  means something.
- **Substitutes must name AI-driven substitution explicitly and assess
  it.** In most knowledge industries it is now *the* substitute threat,
  and a five forces read that omits it is describing 2015.
- **Supplier power must include cloud, model, and platform
  dependencies.** Supplier concentration in this decade looks like a
  single inference provider, not a single parts vendor.
- **End on the profit pool.** Where does the money in this industry
  actually accumulate, and does the structure let you reach it? That is
  the output. The five ratings are the work.

## Ansoff Growth Options

Four quadrants, evidence per quadrant, risk gradient respected.

1. **Market penetration** -- existing product, existing market. Lowest
   risk.
2. **Market development** -- existing product, new market.
3. **Product development** -- new product, existing market.
4. **Diversification** -- new product, new market. Highest risk.

Two to three candidate moves per quadrant, each with a risk rating and
the justification for that rating. **An empty quadrant is an acceptable
answer**, and it is usually diversification. Inventing a
diversification play to fill the box is how a growth plan acquires a
liability.

The risk gradient is not decorative. A recommendation that puts
diversification first needs to explain why penetration is exhausted,
with evidence that it is.

Close on a **recommended sequence**, not a ranked list. Sequence
implies dependency, which is what a plan is. Then two more lines:

- **Not yet:** the tempting move, and why the evidence says wait.
- **The assumption that breaks this sequence** if it turns out wrong.

## Battle Card Builder

Evidence-cited, field-action format. A card salespeople will not use is
a document, not a card -- and a card that reads like a briefing instead
of a sales tool never gets opened at all, however well-sourced it is.

Structure:

1. **One-page field summary** -- the page a rep actually opens, built
   only from material earned in the sections below it
2. **Thirty-second read** -- a wedge, not a biography: who they are,
   when you win, when you lose, framed as the tension their strength
   creates for this buyer
3. **Top 5 competitor weaknesses** -- ranked against a stated basis,
   each with its evidence ref
4. **Top 5 advantages** -- max 5 points, outcome language, not feature
   lists
5. **Ask this** -- max 3 trap questions the competitor's architecture or
   pricing cannot answer well. Never ask what you cannot evidence: a
   trap question you cannot back up traps your own rep.
6. **Watch out for** -- their strongest true claims, stated honestly,
   with your response
7. **If they say / you say** -- a memorizable call-and-response grid for
   the claims they will actually make in the room
8. **Pricing and packaging snapshot** -- with a capture date
9. **Do not say** -- claims that are tempting, plausible, and not
   supported
10. **Appendix: evidence table** -- every claim, source URL, date, label

**Rules:**

- Every claim on the card traces to a row in the evidence table. An
  assertion without a source and date is an opinion wearing a badge.
- A "Top 5" list orders by a stated basis -- deal frequency, dollar
  impact, review-complaint frequency -- named once, not by vibes.
- Date-stamp the card and name its decay: the pricing and packaging
  layer goes stale in weeks, the positioning layer in quarters.
- The "do not say" section is the one that keeps a rep from being
  corrected in front of a customer. It is not optional padding.
- Never build the card from feature parity. Build it from which
  customer problem each side actually solves.
- Discipline vocabulary (OSINT, SIGINT, FININT, and the rest) stays in
  this doctrine and in the skill's own teaching prose. None of it
  belongs in the card itself -- a rep does not say "per our SIGINT
  sweep" to a buyer.

Freshest inputs: pricing, packaging, and messaging signals, plus win/loss
interviews. Public review mining supplies the objection handling.

## Positioning

The say versus said-about gap from the OSINT sweep is the raw material:
their positioning language minus their customers' language.

- Where the two match, that is defended ground. Do not attack it.
- Where they diverge, that is the exposed flank.
- Where the customer language has no vendor claiming it, that is
  whitespace worth naming.

Hand the gap to a positioning skill for the statement itself. What
belongs here is the evidence: sourced quotes on both sides, with dates.

## PESTEL

Political, economic, social, technological, environmental, legal.
External forces only.

Run it as a **delta** after the first pass. The first PESTEL is a
baseline; the value is in what moved. See `monitors.md` for the delta
schema.

Discipline rule: each factor either moved materially or it did not.
"No material movement" is a valid and useful entry. A PESTEL where all
six factors moved every quarter is not monitoring, it is horoscope
writing.

## Choosing Between Them

| The question | The framework |
|---|---|
| Who is even in this market? | Landscape scan |
| Who should we worry about? | Competitive snapshot |
| What do customers actually need? | Voice-of-customer mining |
| How big is this? | TAM/SAM/SOM |
| Where do we stand? | SWOT |
| Is this industry worth being in? | Five Forces |
| Where do we grow next? | Ansoff |
| What does the field say tomorrow? | Battle card |
| What do we claim, and against whom? | Positioning |
| What changed outside our control? | PESTEL delta |
