# Weak Example: Twenty Boxes, All Full

**Synthetic anti-pattern.** Intentionally flawed, and the flaws are hard to spot. Four quadrants, five entries each, most with sources, and a set of crossings at the end. It follows the template exactly. It would pass a quick review — it was presented at a planning offsite and shaped a roadmap.

Same fictional company, analyzed by a different analyst.

## The Artifact

### Strengths

1. Strong engineering team
2. Published pricing
3. SOC 2 Type II certified
4. Two native ERP connectors
5. Customer-centric culture

### Weaknesses

1. Cartelane's implementation is faster than ours
2. Limited connector coverage compared to competitors
3. No mobile application
4. Smaller marketing budget than Meridian
5. Technical debt in the reporting module

### Opportunities

1. Expand into regulated verticals
2. Build out the partner ecosystem
3. Growing revenue operations market ($12.6B, 18% CAGR)
4. Launch a self-serve tier
5. AI-driven insights

### Threats

1. Cartelane's integration platform
2. Economic uncertainty
3. Larger competitors with more resources
4. Increasing customer expectations
5. Talent competition

### Crossings

- **S-O:** Our SOC 2 certification supports expansion into regulated verticals.
- **W-T:** Limited connector coverage is exposed by Cartelane's integration platform. We should accelerate connector development.

## Why It Passes a Quick Read

- All four quadrants populated, with crossings at the end — which most SWOTs omit entirely.
- Several entries carry real, verifiable facts: the SOC 2 certification, the published pricing, the two connectors.
- The market-size figure has a source and a growth rate.
- The crossings section exists and produces a concrete instruction.
- Nothing is fabricated outright.
- It fits on one slide, which is how a SWOT actually gets used.

## Why It Fails

**Weakness 1 is a threat.** "Cartelane's implementation is faster than ours" describes a competitor's capability. It is theirs, not ours. *This is the quadrant violation signal verbatim.* The weakness underneath — our six-week onboarding against an RFP criterion — is a different sentence with different evidence and a different owner, and it never gets written. So the thing we could actually fix stays invisible while the quadrant looks addressed.

Weakness 4, "smaller marketing budget than Meridian," makes the same error in reverse: it is a comparison, not a condition, and it is not actionable at any altitude below the CFO.

**Three of five opportunities are strategies.** "Expand into regulated verticals," "build out the partner ecosystem," and "launch a self-serve tier" all begin with a verb we control. They are things we might do. The external conditions that would make them viable — a compliance regime creating demand, a competitor vacating a price segment, a standard lowering switching costs — are absent. *This is the opportunity violation signal three times over*, and it is the mechanism by which a SWOT becomes a wish list: the plan gets filed as the evidence for itself.

"AI-driven insights" is not an opportunity at all. It is a technology.

**Every quadrant has exactly five entries.** Twenty boxes, twenty filled. The correctly-run version has three strengths, three weaknesses, three opportunities, four threats, and one explicit "no evidence found" — because that is what the evidence supported. Here, "strong engineering team" and "customer-centric culture" are unfalsifiable self-descriptions that appear on nearly every SWOT ever produced, and they are here because the template had five rows.

**No entry is ranked, and no basis is stated.** A two-year certification moat sits beside "published pricing," which a single competitor decision could erase. "Economic uncertainty" sits beside a specific, dated, four-discipline threat. Without ranking on defensibility, exploitability, fit, and likelihood-times-damage, every entry reads as equally weighted — and the threat that ends the category (adjacent CRM vendors owning the sales-side data) does not appear at all, because it was neither urgent nor top-of-mind.

**Weaknesses came from the backlog, not from customers.** "No mobile application," "technical debt in the reporting module," "limited connector coverage" — this is an engineering to-do list. Not one entry carries a customer quote, a win/loss line, or a support theme. Meanwhile the strongest customer-evidenced weakness in the evidence base, *"three weeks with them, six with you — that's a whole close cycle,"* is nowhere. Teams are wrong about their own weaknesses in a predictable direction, and this is precisely that direction.

**Threats 2 through 5 are ambient.** "Economic uncertainty," "larger competitors with more resources," "increasing customer expectations," "talent competition." Every one is true of every company in every year. They cannot be ranked, monitored, or acted upon, and they crowd out the specific dated threats the evidence actually supports.

**The W-T crossing recommends the wrong move.** "Accelerate connector development" races a competitor who has eleven endpoints staged and a dedicated team, from a base of two. The evidence supports a concession — deepen the two we have into the audit story, where their in-process SOC 2 cannot follow. A crossing that produces only offensive moves has not taken the threat seriously, and this one commits a quarter of engineering to a race already lost.

**The market-size figure is the larger of two conflicting analyst estimates**, adopted without its 3.1x twin.

## What Makes This Hard to Catch

Completeness is the disguise, and in a SWOT it is nearly total. The failure mode everyone watches for is an empty box, and there are none. A reviewer scans twenty populated cells and reads diligence.

The quadrant errors are individually invisible. "Cartelane's implementation is faster than ours" is a true, sourceable, decision-relevant sentence. It belongs in the document. It is simply in the wrong box, and being in the wrong box means the actionable version of it never gets written — a loss that leaves no trace.

The strategies-as-opportunities failure is the most seductive of all, because a quadrant full of things we might do *feels* like strategic thinking, and reads as ambition to an executive audience. It is the version of the artifact everyone in the room prefers.

## Repair

Move "Cartelane's implementation is faster" to threats, then write the weakness underneath it in our own terms — six weeks to first reconciled report — with the win/loss quote attached. Show the correction in the document; it teaches the rule better than a footnote.

Strip the strategies out of opportunities and replace them with external conditions: the vacated price segment with its dated pricing capture, the interchange standard, the growing and better-paid buyer population. If a strategy survives the deletion, it belongs in the crossings, which is where plans go.

Let the quadrants be short. Delete "strong engineering team" and "customer-centric culture" for lack of evidence, and add an explicit "no evidence found" wherever a gap remains — starting with Northwind, who appeared in three lost deals and is characterized nowhere.

Rank every quadrant on its stated basis, and watch the adjacent-CRM threat appear once likelihood-times-damage is applied rather than urgency. Rebuild weaknesses from customer evidence rather than the backlog. Then rewrite the W-T crossing as the concession the evidence supports, and write down that it is a concession — which is the sentence the offsite most needed to hear.
