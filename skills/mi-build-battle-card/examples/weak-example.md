# Weak Example: The Card That Got a Rep Corrected

**Synthetic anti-pattern.** Intentionally flawed, and the flaws are hard to spot. It has a one-page summary, a thirty-second read, talk tracks, trap questions, a pricing table, and confident positioning. It is well organized and the field liked it. It would pass a quick review — and a prospect corrected a rep from it in week two.

Same fictional Cartelane case, built by a different analyst.

## The Artifact

### One-Page Summary

**The wedge:** Our FININT and SIGINT sweeps show Cartelane is under real financial pressure, and our OSINT read on their messaging confirms they are struggling to differentiate.

**Their top weaknesses:** Weak integration story, deteriorating support, aggressive pricing that will not last.

**Why we win:** Compliance, reliability, and depth — full stop.

### Top 5 Cartelane Weaknesses

1. Weak integration story
2. Deteriorating support
3. Overpriced for what you get
4. Not built for compliance
5. Losing momentum in the market

### Thirty-Second Read

Cartelane is a venture-backed mid-market competitor with a weak integration story and deteriorating support. We win on reliability, compliance, and depth.

### Top 5 Cartelane Advantages

1. We have certified SOC 2 Type II compliance; they do not
2. Our integration layer is production-proven; theirs is not shipped
3. We offer full field-level audit lineage
4. Our platform is enterprise-grade and battle-tested
5. Customers report significantly better support experiences with us
6. We have the strongest brand in the category

### Ask This

1. "How is your support response time these days?"
2. "Do you have SOC 2 Type II?"
3. "How many native connectors do you have?"
4. "What's your uptime been like this year?"

### Watch Out For

- They may claim fast implementation. Our implementation is more thorough.
- They may claim lower cost. Focus the conversation on total value.

### Pricing

Growth $119/user/mo, Scale $189/user/mo, Enterprise custom.

### Competitive Positioning

Cartelane competes on speed and price. We compete on trust and depth. In any deal where compliance or reliability matters, we should win.

## Why It Passes a Quick Read

- Structured in the recognizable battle card shape, with all the expected sections, including the newer one-page summary and Top 5 lists.
- The one-page summary reads confidently and covers the essentials in one skim — exactly what makes it the page reps actually use.
- The pricing figures are accurate as of the day it was written.
- The SOC 2 and integration points are real, verifiable advantages.
- Talk tracks are punchy and confident — the field will read and use them.
- It gives clear, usable trap questions.
- Nothing in it is fabricated out of nothing.

## Why It Fails

**There is no evidence appendix and no dates anywhere.** Not one claim traces to a source. *This is the discipline's central violation signal*, and it has two consequences: a rep challenged with "where did you hear that?" has nothing, and the next refresh cannot tell which lines are stale. Six weeks later, the pricing table was still being quoted after another change.

**The one-page summary — the page most reps will actually read — talks like a briefing document, not a person.** "Our FININT and SIGINT sweeps show..." and "our OSINT read on their messaging" are discipline vocabulary that does real work in an analyst's own notes, and none in the sentence a rep says out loud to a buyer. No rep will ever say "per our SIGINT sweep" in a room, and a summary that talks like the process instead of the point trains the field to skim past exactly the page meant to save them from reading everything else. *This is the violation signal for speaking like a seller, not a sensor:* a discipline acronym anywhere in the one-page summary, the thirty-second read, or the Top 5 lists.

**The Top 5 Weaknesses list has no stated ranking basis and no evidence refs.** "Weak integration story," "deteriorating support," and "losing momentum in the market" read like a confident, organized list — but nothing says why these five, in this order, or what any of them trace back to. Two of them ("deteriorating support," in the sense reps mean it, and "losing momentum") are claims the fuller card elsewhere shows are unsupported or wrong. *This is the ranking-basis violation signal:* a Top 5 list is not evidence-backed just because it is numbered and looks organized — the order is a claim too, and here it has neither a stated basis nor a citation underneath it.

**There is no Do Not Say section**, and four claims that belong in it are on the card instead:

- **"Deteriorating support"** rests on a public-forum sample, not on contracted SLAs. The prospect who corrected the rep was a happy Cartelane customer with a four-hour SLA.
- **"Customers report significantly better support experiences with us"** is a win-reason claim, and win/loss has been unverified for eight months.
- **"Enterprise-grade and battle-tested"** and **"the strongest brand in the category"** are unfalsifiable and evidence nothing.
- The read calls their integration story **"weak"** when eleven endpoints are staged and a launch certificate is live — it is unreleased, not weak, and the distinction will be visible within two quarters.

**Two trap questions cannot be evidenced, and one traps our own rep.** "How is your support response time these days?" invites "our contracted SLA is four hours and we meet it," and the rep has nothing. "What's your uptime been like this year?" invites the prospect to compare status pages, which does not favor us. *This is the trap-question violation signal exactly:* questions with no evidence row and no anticipated counter. There are also four questions, where the cap is three — and the fourth is the worst of them.

**"Watch out for" contains no true competitor strength.** "They may claim fast implementation. Our implementation is more thorough" reframes their genuine advantage as a flaw. It is corroborated in seven of nineteen reviews and it is *real*: three weeks against our six, scored in four of four RFPs. A rep told to dismiss it will contest a true claim, lose credibility, and lose the room — which is precisely what happened.

**The thirty-second read never says when we lose.** It lists three things we win on and nothing else. A card that never states the losing condition is fiction, and experienced reps discount the whole document once they notice.

**The renamed "Top 5 Advantages" section has six items where the cap is five, and the language is capabilities, not outcomes.** "We offer full field-level audit lineage" describes a feature; "your auditors accept the number without a screenshot trail" describes what the buyer gets. Reps deliver the first as a spec, and buyers hear a spec.

**The pricing table has no capture date and no change history.** Cartelane removed a $49 entry tier this quarter, raising the floor 144%, and widened its annual discount from 10% to 20%. All of that is competitively significant — an entire segment of their base was just repriced and is now addressable — and none of it is here.

**"In any deal where compliance or reliability matters, we should win"** is a conclusion the evidence does not support, stated in a way that gives a rep no handle on the deals where it is not true.

## What Makes This Hard to Catch

It is a *good-feeling* card. Confident, clean, and the field liked it — adoption is the metric battle cards are usually judged on, and this one was adopted immediately. The one-page summary in particular looks like exactly the artifact this skill is supposed to produce: short, skimmable, organized into the right-looking sections. The jargon in it reads as competence to anyone who is not the audience it is written for — a reviewer familiar with the discipline vocabulary will nod right past "our SIGINT sweep shows," because it sounds like rigor, not because it is usable.

The failure only surfaces in a live call, where nobody from marketing is present, and it comes back as "the customer pushed back and I had nothing," which is easy to attribute to the rep.

The claims are also not fabricated. Every one has a real observation somewhere behind it, stretched by a degree or two: a measured forum sample becomes "deteriorating," a staged capability becomes "weak," an unverified impression becomes "customers report." Each stretch is small. The card is the sum of them.

And the missing sections leave no gaps. A card with no Do Not Say section does not look like a card with something missing. It looks like a card.

## Repair

Build the evidence appendix first and put every claim through it. Whatever fails to earn a row does not go on the card — that step alone moves four current claims into Do Not Say, which is where the run's real value is.

Rewrite the one-page summary in plain, sayable language, and only from what the fuller card below it already supports: no "FININT," "SIGINT," or "OSINT" anywhere in it, and no weakness or advantage claim that cannot be found, with its evidence ref, further down the card.

Give the Top 5 Weaknesses list a stated basis — this run would rank by dollar impact and review-complaint frequency — and cut any entry ("losing momentum," "not built for compliance") that cannot earn an evidence ref under that basis.

Rewrite the thirty-second read to state when we lose, specifically: speed to first report, three weeks against six, scored in four of four RFPs. Reps will trust the rest of the card because of that sentence.

Cut "Top 5 Advantages" to five and rewrite each as an outcome the buyer gets, dropping the unfalsifiable "strongest brand" entry entirely. Cut "ask this" to three, drop the two that cannot be evidenced, and give each survivor its evidence row and its expected counter.

Rebuild "watch out for" around their genuine strengths — implementation speed first — and concede it plainly, then supply the reframe. Add the pricing capture date and the change history, because the removed entry tier is a campaign, not a footnote. Then date-stamp the card, name the decay by layer, and schedule the weekly pricing and packaging watch that keeps the fastest-rotting half honest.
