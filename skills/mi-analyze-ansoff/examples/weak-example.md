# Weak Example: Twelve Moves and a Numbered List

**Synthetic anti-pattern.** Intentionally flawed, and the flaws are hard to spot. Four quadrants, three moves each, risk ratings throughout, and a prioritized recommendation. It looks like a complete growth plan. It would pass a quick review — and it was funded.

Same fictional company, analyzed by a different analyst.

## The Artifact

### Current Position

Mid-market revenue operations software.

### 1. Market Penetration — low risk

- Increase outbound volume
- Improve conversion rates
- Expand within existing accounts

*Our mid-market segment is increasingly saturated, with eleven vendors competing for the same accounts. Growth here is likely to be incremental.*

### 2. Market Development — medium risk

- Enter Germany
- Enter the UK
- Move upmarket to enterprise

### 3. Product Development — medium risk

- Add more ERP connectors
- Build AI-driven insights
- Add a mobile application

### 4. Diversification — high risk

- Compliance reporting for regulated healthcare
- Expand into procurement analytics
- Partner-led services offering

### Recommendation

1. Enter Germany — largest addressable opportunity
2. Compliance reporting for healthcare — highest strategic upside
3. Add ERP connectors — competitive necessity
4. Continue penetration efforts

## Why It Passes a Quick Read

- All four quadrants populated with three moves each — visually complete and balanced.
- Risk levels are labeled per quadrant, in the correct order.
- The moves are recognizable, plausible, and the sort of thing this company could actually attempt.
- It ends with a clear prioritized recommendation.
- Nothing is fabricated outright.

## Why It Fails

**Penetration was dismissed in one sentence with no denominator anywhere.** "Increasingly saturated, with eleven vendors competing for the same accounts" counts *vendors*, not *unsold buyers*. The actual figure is 0.9% — 340 customers against 38,400 eligible establishments, with a 31% win rate when contested. *This is the penetration violation signal exactly*, and it is the error the entire plan rests on: every subsequent recommendation follows from a saturation claim that the available data contradicts by two orders of magnitude.

**The penetration moves are not moves.** "Increase outbound volume," "improve conversion rates," "expand within existing accounts" are activities every company performs continuously. None names a specific opportunity, an evidenced trigger, or a customer. The three real ones sitting in the evidence base — a competitor's abandoned price segment, a lost RFP criterion, a stranded partner-connector population — are all absent, so the quadrant reads empty even though it is the fullest one.

**Diversification was filled, and one of its entries got funded.** Three moves in the highest-risk quadrant, none with a citation, in a document where nothing else in five sweeps mentions healthcare, procurement, or services economics. *This is the empty-quadrant violation signal*, and it is the most expensive one in the library: a fabricated entry here is not padding, it is a proposal, and it was ranked second in the recommendation.

**Every quadrant has exactly three moves.** Twelve boxes, twelve filled. The correctly-run version has three, two, two, and zero — because that is what the evidence supported.

**Risk is rated by quadrant, not by move, and no unknown is ever named.** "Medium risk" applied to both Germany and the UK conceals the fact that Germany's risk is specific and nameable: *the buyer title does not exist there*. No German statistical classification covers revenue operations; the duties are distributed across Controlling and sales leadership. That single unknown is what makes Germany high risk rather than medium, and naming it would have converted an expensive entry into a cheap research question — ten interviews, one week. Instead Germany is ranked first.

**The recommendation is a ranked list, not a sequence.** Four numbered items, no dependencies, no timeframes, no statement of what funds what or what must be learned before what. "Enter Germany" and "compliance reporting for healthcare" are both first-year commitments with no revenue-generating move ahead of them. Nothing indicates what happens if move 1 takes longer than planned, because nothing connects the moves at all.

**It leads with market development and follows with diversification, while penetration is ranked fourth.** The risk gradient is inverted and no burden of proof is discharged — the saturation claim was supposed to do that work, and it is unsupported.

**There is no "not yet" and no breaking assumption.** So the healthcare proposal is never actually assessed, merely ranked; and the plan carries no condition that would tell anyone it had stopped being right. It cannot be monitored, only executed or abandoned.

## What Makes This Hard to Catch

The grid is the disguise. A four-quadrant matrix with three items per box is the visual form of a complete analysis, and reviewers check for empty boxes rather than for unsupported entries. Symmetry reads as diligence in exactly the framework where asymmetry is the finding.

"Increasingly saturated" is the sentence that does all the damage, and it is unremarkable. It sounds like market awareness. It is the kind of thing everyone in the room already believes, which is precisely why it goes unchallenged, and checking it requires a denominator that nobody thinks to ask for during a growth discussion.

And the recommendation is exciting. Germany and healthcare are new, big, and strategic. "Cut onboarding by two weeks and call a competitor's abandoned customers" is none of those things, and it is the plan the evidence supports.

## Repair

Run the penetration check first, with a real denominator, and report the number even though it will deflate the meeting. Then rebuild the penetration quadrant from the evidence base — the price refugees, the RFP criterion, the stranded partner customers — which is where three low-risk, evidenced moves were sitting the whole time.

Empty the diversification quadrant and mark it "no evidence found," then address the healthcare proposal in a "not yet" section with the specific evidence that says wait and the condition that would change it. That is how a proposal gets assessed rather than merely deferred.

Rate each move individually and name the unknown behind every rating. Germany's unknown is the buyer title, and naming it turns a market entry into a week of interviews.

Replace the ranked list with a sequence: what funds what, what teaches what, what must complete before what begins. Then write the assumption that breaks the sequence — here, that penetration remains open — and say how you would notice if it stopped being true.
